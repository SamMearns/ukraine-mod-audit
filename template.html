Introduction

Why this matters

Ukraine's General Staff has published a running count of Russian losses every day since 24 February 2022. It now stands at 1.6 million. Newsrooms, think tanks and politicians repeat it as fact.

It is a wartime claim by one side, about the other side, released with no breakdown and no way to check it. That does not make it false. It does make it unsafe to build analysis on without testing it first.

This page puts the figure through ten tests: seasons, front size, weather, battles, doctrine and firepower, Russian recruitment, independent death counts, officials' own statements, the incentives behind the number and the drone footage it rests on. Every check uses data from outside Ukraine. The judgements below say how likely each conclusion is, using the scale UK Defence Intelligence uses for its own assessments.

Bottom line

Key judgements

The judgements below use the probability yardstick that UK Defence Intelligence uses in its own assessments, so each one states how likely it is, not only what we found.

  1. A

    It is highly likely that the daily figure is not a day-by-day measurement of Russian losses. It does not move with rain, snow, cold or a lagged day, and on the most variable test (wind) the effect is about 4%. Since mid-2024 none of 14 major battles, including Avdiivka's final assault, Kursk and Pokrovsk, pushed it outside an ordinary month's range. Using it for daily, weather or single-battle analysis builds conclusions on numbers that cannot carry them.

  2. B

    It is likely that the cumulative total is the right order of magnitude for killed plus wounded. It sits 3.1 to 4.8 times above Russian-sourced and CSIS death estimates at every matched date, which implies 2.1 to 3.8 wounded per death, inside the normal historical range. Western estimates that match it almost exactly are mostly an echo, because they draw on Ukrainian data. The fully independent check, deaths from Russia's own registries (including a leaked Rosstat register of 450,100 deaths), is consistent with anything from about 1.15 to 1.6 million today, depending on how many video-claimed drone hits were real (Test 10).

  3. C

    It is a realistic possibility that figures since mid-2026 are inflated. Claimed losses reached records while ISW shows Russia gaining about 80 km² net in ten months. The defence ministry's own video-verified drone count stayed flat at about 30,000 a month while the published total rose to 54,200, and October became the first month to clear the 50,000 target set in January. Against this, a NATO official put September's Russian losses even higher than the General Staff did.

  4. D

    It is almost certain that comparing 1.6 million to Mediazona's 283,000 as like for like is an error, in either direction. One counts casualties, the other counts named dead, and the gap between them is mostly the wounded.

Test 1The official series

The whole series, and season by season

The General Staff figure has risen in three steps: about 200 a day in mid-2022, 600–1,000 through 2023, and 1,000–1,500 from 2024. In 2026 it climbed again, averaging 1,750 a day in October and over 2,200 in early November, the highest sustained level of the war. Fighting in Ukraine is known to follow the ground. Mud in spring (rasputitsa) and late autumn slows armour and assaults, and summer and frozen winter ground favour them.

Assessment The season changes how steady the number is, not how big it is.

Spring mud never produces the lowest season. Since 2024, summers have been twice as steady as winters (7.5% daily variation against 18%), the opposite of what bursty summer fighting should produce.

Test 2Front-line size and intensity

How consistent should the numbers be on a 1,200 km front?

No source outside Ukraine publishes a measured length for the active front. Ukraine's command gives 1,200–1,250 km (reported by AP, September 2025, unverified). Independent analysts' maps give about 1,000–1,100 km. The model below works with any figure in that range, and the answer barely changes across it. A long front works against the "too consistent" argument. A daily total built from hundreds of separate sectors should be fairly smooth, in the same way a national daily birth count is smoother than one hospital's. The real question is how smooth. The model below adds up independent sectors and lets you set how clustered the losses are and how much front-wide events (weather, big offensives) move them.

Assessment Smoothness is expected. Ignoring the battlefield is not.

A 1,100 km front explains about 15% day-to-day variation, but not summer's 7.5%. Monthly claims show no link to ISW's map changes in 2024–25 (ρ = 0.00). In 2026 they hit records while Russia gained about 80 km² net. Russia kept attacking elsewhere and drones hit defenders too, so lost ground alone does not prove inflation.

Test 3Weather

Does the weather move the number?

Daily weather comes from five front-line towns (Pokrovsk, Bakhmut, Kupiansk, Orikhiv and Kherson) via the Open-Meteo historical archive. Each day's claim is compared with its own 29-day median, so the test looks only at day-to-day movement. Correlation with the detrended daily claim (Spearman ρ, n = 1,404): rainfall −0.05, snowfall −0.08, temperature −0.02, cloud cover +0.04, wind −0.12. Rain against the next day's claim: −0.003, so a reporting lag does not hide the effect.

Assessment Weather does not move the daily number.

Rain, snow and frost have no measurable effect. Wind, which grounds drones, shifts it by about 4%. The daily figure cannot support day-level analysis.

Test 4Key battles

Do the big battles show up in the count?

A real casualty count should jump when a set-piece battle peaks, and dip when a truce holds. Battle dates come from ISW, CNN, Al Jazeera, AFP, Meduza and other sources outside Ukraine. For each battle's most intense phase, the claimed daily average is compared with the six weeks before it, then ranked against every other stretch of the same length since June 2022.

Assessment Since mid-2024, battles no longer show up in the count.

Before August 2024, 5 of 10 battles pushed claims into the top 10% of all periods. Since then none of 14 has, including Avdiivka, Kursk and Pokrovsk. Most truces left it flat too.

Test 5Doctrine and firepower

Does the count follow who has the guns, and who is attacking?

Two military explanations could make an honest count look strange. Firepower: Russian losses should rise when Ukraine's ability to kill rises. That ability went from a shell shortage in early 2024 to drone dominance in 2025–26. Posture: the old rule that attackers lose more is weaker than it sounds. Across hundreds of historical battles, the Dupuy Institute found attackers' losses are on average similar to defenders'. Only failed attacks cost the attacker clearly more, about 1.7 to 1. The famous 3:1 rule is about how many troops an attack needs, not about who loses more.

Assessment Doctrine explains the trend. It does not explain the blind spots.

The 2025 dip fits Russia's switch to 2–5-man infiltration groups, which exposed fewer men while taking more ground. The 2026 rise fits a drone kill zone that RUSI puts 30 km deep, which hits defenders as hard as attackers. Neither explains a count that stopped reacting to battles in 2024, or a 2026 rise that sits entirely outside the video-verified data. RUSI found that only about half of Ukraine's publicly claimed drone kills of Russian artillery were real destructions.

Test 6Recruitment and mobilisation

Can Russia replace what Ukraine says it loses?

Russia mobilised 300,000 reservists in autumn 2022 and has relied on paid contract recruitment since. Official figures come from Dmitry Medvedev and the defence ministry. Independent estimates come from regional enlistment-bonus spending (Janis Kluge, SWP; iStories). Vladimir Putin has put the Russian force in the war zone at 617,000 (December 2023), almost 700,000 (June 2024), 700,000 (September and December 2025) and more than 700,000 (June 2026). Since mid-2024, then, Russia's own figure has the force roughly flat.

Test 7Independent counts

Cross-checking against Mediazona and Meduza

Mediazona and BBC Russian confirm deaths one name at a time from obituaries, social media and leaked registries. It is a hard floor, and it lags by months. Separately, Mediazona and Meduza estimate total deaths from Russia's probate registry, which shows excess deaths among men of fighting age. Neither counts the wounded. Ukraine's figure does.

Assessment Against the right comparison, the total holds up.

Ukraine's figure stays 3.4–4.8 times Russian-sourced death estimates, a normal ratio for killed plus wounded. 1.6 million is casualties, not deaths, and 283,000 is a floor, not the toll. CSIS matches the total to within 1%, which may reflect shared inputs.

Test 8Officials' statements

What officials said against what was published

Ukrainian commanders and ministers regularly give their own figures for Russian losses, for a single battle, a month or a year. These statements are the thing being tested, so they come from Ukrainian officials by definition. Each is checked two ways: against the General Staff's own daily totals for the same dates, and against figures for the same battle from governments and researchers outside Ukraine.

Test 9Incentives

What happens when the number becomes a target

In January 2026 Ukraine's defence minister Mykhailo Fedorov named 50,000 Russian casualties a month a strategic goal, as reported by Business Insider and Al Jazeera. Under a points system, drone units earn credits, spent on new equipment, for each kill confirmed on video. Video confirmation is a real safeguard. A public target plus a reward for each counted kill is still the textbook setting for Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure.

Test 10Footage verification

What if a share of the video-confirmed hits weren't hits?

Since 2025, most of the figure rests on drone footage. Outside sources put drones behind 70–80% of casualties, and Ukraine's own video-verified drone count equals 63–107% of the monthly total in 2026. An FPV drone's video ends when its camera dies. That happens on a direct hit, but also when the drone strikes the ground a few metres short, is jammed in the last seconds, or the target dives clear. From the strike drone's own feed you cannot tell these apart, or tell killed from wounded. Only a second camera watching the aftermath settles it.

Ukraine's defence ministry says every counted strike is verified on video inside its own systems. It has not published the standard, and no outside party audits it. The one outside measurement comes from RUSI, which found that only about half of Ukraine's publicly claimed drone kills of Russian artillery were real destructions.

Audit protocol: measure the hit rate yourself

Sample strike videos posted by Ukrainian unit channels and code every strike once. The share of A and B gives a measured hit rate to put in the slider. Published clips are each unit's best footage, so the result is an upper bound.

CodeWhat the clip showsCounts as
AA second camera shows the aftermath: the person down, not moving, or being evacuatedConfirmed casualty
BThe strike feed shows detonation on or within about 1 m of the person; no aftermath shownProbable casualty (count as half)
CFeed ends on final approach with the target still moving or more than about 2 m awayUnconfirmed
DFeed breaks up early (jamming) or the drone crashes before reaching the targetMiss
ESame person, place and time as another clip already codedDuplicate (exclude)

Also record the unit, date, sector, drone type, the distance to the target in the last clear frame, and how many drones struck the same target. Hit rate ≈ (A + 0.5 × B) ÷ (A + B + C + D).

Assessment The total is only as good as footage nobody outside Ukraine audits.

If half the video-claimed drone hits were real casualties, the rate RUSI found for artillery, Russian losses today would be about 1.15 million, not 1.6 million. Russia's own death records could not tell the difference: the ratio of wounded to dead would still be believable in a drone war. Russia's death records only rule out hit rates below about 30%. Treat the total as a range of about 1.15–1.6 million.

For analysts

Where the confirmation bias actually sits

Quote it as a claim, with its definition

"Ukraine's General Staff says Russia has lost about 1.6 million personnel killed, wounded or captured." Not "Russia has lost 1.6 million soldiers."

Never analyse the daily number

It is rounded, smoothed and blind to weather. Daily spikes and dips say more about reporting than about battles. Use monthly totals at the finest.

Trace every number to its source

A Western or think-tank total that matches Ukraine's may simply be Ukraine's. Ask where an estimate gets its data before counting it as confirmation.

Compare like with like

Casualties go against casualties (CSIS, UK Defence Intelligence). Deaths go against deaths (Mediazona, Meduza). Mixing them manufactures either a massacre or a cover-up.

Treat the latest six months as provisional

Independent counts lag. The months with no outside check are the months where incentives now act most strongly on the figure.

Check it against the map

Real losses should track real fighting. When claimed losses and ground changing hands move apart, ask why before quoting.

Hold the sceptical case to the same standard

The Ukrainian total has matched independent casualty estimates for three years. "Ukraine inflates everything" is as much a confirmation bias as "Ukraine's numbers are fact."

Year by yearUkraine MOD · Mediazona · Rosstat leak

Each year of the war, three counts side by side

In November 2026, two hacker groups opposed to the Kremlin, Belarusian Cyber Partisans and Silent Crow, published what they say is Rosstat's internal register of death certificates up to 28 August 2026. It counts 450,100 military-related deaths, 393,881 of them explicitly coded to military operations. Rosstat admits a cyberattack but denies any data was taken. The Moscow Times checked a sample of names against Mediazona's list but could not verify the whole set. The leak did not come through Ukrainian intelligence, but it is unverified, and its war-death code also covers some civilians killed by shelling inside Russia.

Ukraine's figure counts killed, wounded and captured. The leak and Mediazona count deaths. So the result for each year is the ratio between them: how many claimed casualties there are for each registered death. Historically, wars run at 3.5 to 5 casualties per death. In a drone war, where the wounded wait days to be evacuated, the ratio can fall lower.

Method

Data and sources

All statistics were computed from the raw series: 1,716 daily General Staff figures (24 Feb 2022 – 6 Nov 2026), Open-Meteo daily weather averaged over five front-line towns, and AFP's monthly analyses of ISW maps (2024–2026). The General Staff series and officials' statements are the subject under test, so they are necessarily Ukrainian. Every other input comes from outside Ukraine; DeepState appears only as a comparison. Purported Russian documents released by Ukrainian intelligence (an alleged Kremlin loss assessment, March 2026, and an alleged Russian loss sheet, October 2025) are excluded, because their provenance runs through a party to the war. Daily tests use 2023 onward, after the war of movement ended. Correlations are Spearman rank correlations. Recruitment, death and battle figures are as published by their sources and carry their sources' uncertainty.
